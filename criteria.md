# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---

## The test set
 
Criteria 1–4 are checked on a fixed set of texts that I collect now, before
building anything, and do not change afterwards:
 
| Group | How many | Where they come from |
|---|---|---|
| Human — my own writing | 6 | Pieces I wrote myself, without AI help |
| Human — plain English | 6 | Short, simply written pieces by other people (classmates, public forums, English-learner writing), not written by AI |
| AI — unedited | 10 | Generated by at least 2 different AI tools with different prompts, pasted in without edits |
 
**Frozen before any tuning.** In Milestone 4 I set my weights, the detector midpoint and my thresholds using a *different* set of calibration texts. I don't score these 22 test pieces until unit 8, so no setting is fitted to them and the criteria can't be passed by tuning to the test.
 
**Trials.** Unit 8 runs everything three times, and a target counts as met only if it holds in all three.

---

## 1.

Of 12 human-written pieces in my frozen test set (6 I wrote myself, 6 written in plain, simple English by other people), **0** of the 6 plain pieces and at most **1** of all 12 are labelled high-confidence AI, in every one of the three trials.

**Why this target:**

My false-positive trace showed plain writing like Minh's can score around 0.7 on signal one, and plain writers are the people least able to argue back. So I hold plain writing to zero false accusations, not "few." The test set is frozen before I set any thresholds, so I can't pass by tuning to it.

---

## 2.

Of the same 12 human pieces, at least **7** are labelled high-confidence human (not "unsure"), in every trial.

**Why this target:**

Criterion 1 could be met by a system that calls everything "unsure." That isn't safe; it just shifts the harm, because every real writer would see a label doubting them. Seven of twelve forces the human label to actually work for clear human writing, which pulls against criterion 1: I can't avoid false accusations just by widening the unsure band.

---

## 3.

Of 10 AI-generated pieces in the frozen test set, produced by at least 2 different AI tools with different prompts and not edited, at least **6** are labelled high-confidence AI, and **none** is labelled high-confidence human, in every trial.

**Why this target:**

A detector that never detects isn't working. I accept that my high AI threshold lets borderline AI text through as "unsure," but six of ten clear, unedited cases should still be caught. Calling unedited AI text "probably written by a person" is a worse failure than "unsure," so that gets a hard zero. Two different tools stops me testing against one model's habits.

---

## 4.

Across all 120 pairs of (AI piece, human piece) from the test set, the AI piece has the higher combined score in at least **85%** of pairs, in every trial.

**Why this target:**

This measures whether the score itself separates AI from human text, regardless of where I put the thresholds. It counts overlap directly, so one extreme score can't hide it the way an average can. 85% is a level where the score is genuinely useful but still realistic for a small local model and simple style measures. If the score fails this, no threshold choice can save the labels.

---

## 5.

All **12** contract checks pass with **0** server errors (500) and `/ping` still answering afterwards. The checks are 7 bad inputs to `/submit` (missing `text`, empty, whitespace only, two words, 50,000 characters, `text` not a string, body not JSON), each returning a 4xx JSON error within 5 seconds, and 5 appeal cases: a valid appeal returns 200, sets the status to `under_review`, and leaves the original decision unchanged in the log; an unknown `content_id` returns 404; missing `reasoning` and empty `reasoning` each return 400; and a second appeal on the same item returns 200 with status still `under_review` and adds no duplicate appeal entry to the log.

**Why this target:**

The detector errors on text under 3 tokens and only reads about 400, so short and huge inputs are known failure points, and next unit's attack set will send them on purpose. A 50,000-character input that hangs for a minute is effectively a crash, hence the 5-second limit. The appeal checks matter because an appeal that silently fails, or rewrites the original decision, breaks the one safeguard a wrongly accused writer has.

# Unit 9 — Capstone criteria

Written on 2026-11-11, before any capstone results. They test the input gate added to `app.py::submit` this unit, its logging, and the bad path. Criteria 1–4 are about the extension; 1, 2 and 4 are about what happens when input is bad.

**Trials.** Everything runs three times with nothing changed in between. A target counts as met only if it holds in all three.

---

## 1.

Of 10 fresh English texts of 20 words or more, each with at least one ASCII control character inserted (codes 0–8, 11, 12, 14–31 or 127; tab, newline and carriage return don't count), **10 of 10** are refused with a 400 and **none** gets a `decided` line in `logs/audit.jsonl`, in every one of the three trials.

**Why this target:**

No person types a null byte or a terminal escape code into a piece of writing. A text carrying one was made or altered by a program, and scoring it gives junk a confident label, which is exactly what the gate exists to stop (MF03 came back "human" in Unit 8). One scored control-character text is one too many, so the target is all ten, not most.

---

## 2.

Of 10 fresh non-English texts of 20 words or more — 5 in non-Latin scripts (Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Hindi, Korean) and 5 in Latin-script languages (Spanish, French, German, Vietnamese, Indonesian), one per language — at least **9 of 10** are refused with a 400, in every one of the three trials.

**Why this target:**

GPT-2 was trained on English, and my style signal only recognises a–z, so a label on another language means nothing, and my README says the service only scores English. I allow one miss, not zero, because a Latin-script language can share enough letters and short words with English that refusing it would need a language detector, which is out of scope. More than one miss means the gate isn't doing what the README says.

---

## 3.

Of 20 fresh, genuinely human short texts (3–30 words, informal, which may include punctuation and up to 6 emoji), written by me or by classmates, **0** are refused by the gate (no `not_english_text` or `too_few_words`), in every one of the three trials.

**Why this target:**

A refused submission gets no `content_id`, and `/appeal` needs one, so a writer the gate wrongly refuses has no way to appeal. That is worse than a wrong label, which at least can be challenged. The gate is meant to stop junk, not short real writing, so the target is zero wrongly refused writers. I allow up to 6 emoji because that is how I actually write short messages (one of my own texts has six), and a gate that only passes emoji-light writing would be tested on a writer who doesn't exist. Texts under 3 words are left out because the gate refuses them by design (`GATE_MIN_WORDS = 3`), so they can't show over-refusal.

---

## 4.

For a fixed set of 10 bad requests to `/submit` — invalid JSON, missing `creator_id`, missing `text`, empty `text`, `text` over 10,000 characters, a punctuation-only text, a one-word text, a rate-limited request (the 6th from one `creator_id` within a minute; the first five aren't counted), and two English texts containing control characters — each produces exactly **one** `rejected` line in `logs/audit.jsonl` with a non-null `reason` and `duration_ms`: **10 of 10**, in every one of the three trials.

**Why this target:**

An attack that shows up only as an absence is one I'll never find. Every bad request has to leave a record that says what was wrong and how long it took, or I can't diagnose it later. A record for nine out of ten still leaves one bad path invisible, so the target is all ten.

---

## 5.

On a warm model (`/health` shows `"detector_loaded": true` before the first timed request), at least **29 of 30** fresh English submissions of 100–400 words finish with `duration_ms` at or under **150 ms**, in every one of the three trials.

**Why this target:**

Measured in Milestone 3 on texts of 106–202 words, a warm submission took 48.2 ms on average and 65.6 ms at most. 150 ms is about twice that worst case, which allows for longer texts (GPT-2 reads up to 400 tokens) without letting a real slowdown through. One slow request in thirty is allowed for a one-off pause on my laptop; more than one means scoring itself is slower than I measured. This is a warm-model criterion; the cold first request (about 4–5.5 s) is not part of it.
